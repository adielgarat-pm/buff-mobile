## 1. ADDitude — blog submission for Awareness Month

**Channel:** `submissions@additudemag.com` — they ask for a **500–800-word sample blog post** on the topic you want to write about; no individual replies promised (writers' guidelines page).
**2026 theme:** *"ADHD at the center: ADHD is complicated and connected. Its diagnosis and treatment should reflect that."* → our angle: the *family* is the connected system; a teen's ADHD shows up as the parent's nervous system at 7:40 a.m.
**Persona / emotion:** P1 Exhausted Morning → Calm; P4 Teen → Hope.
**Title options:** *"I was my son's alarm clock for two years. Here's what finally let me stop."* · *"The morning I stopped correcting and started asking."* · *"What a teenager taught me about the tools we build for him."*

**Draft (≈640 words) — `[REWRITE IN YOUR VOICE BEFORE SENDING]`**

> For two years I was my son's alarm clock. Not the kind you can snooze — the kind that walks into the room, says "it's 7:40," and comes back four minutes later with the same sentence and less patience. He has ADHD. I have a job, a younger child, and a nervous system that had learned to brace at the sound of my own voice.
>
> Awareness Month's theme this year is that ADHD is "complicated and connected," and I want to talk about the least discussed connection: the one between a kid's executive function and a parent's morning. When his brain couldn't start, mine took over. That is not a moral failing on either side. It is just what happens when two nervous systems share a hallway.
>
> We tried everything you have tried. The sticker chart that died with the first sick day. The app with the pet that needed feeding — which worked for six weeks, until he noticed that the pet was the one being fed, not him. A routine app that stopped at age twelve, as if thirteen-year-olds wake up organized.
>
> What changed was not a tool. It was a question I finally asked him instead of a sentence I kept saying: *What do you actually want?* Not what he should want. He said: a specific concert. Then a specific outing with a friend. Real things, chosen by him, that lived outside any app.
>
> Three things followed from that, and they are the only three I would tell another parent.
>
> **One — let the reward be real and let them choose it.** Points are ours; a concert is his. When the goal belongs to the kid, the nagging drops because the *want* is already in the room.
>
> **Two — make the win small and absolute.** We stopped counting how much of the list got done. About three tasks in a day is a good day. Not 100%. Not a percentage at all. Perfectionism is the quiet partner of ADHD, and a bar set at "everything" guarantees a bar never reached.
>
> **Three — plan for the days that collapse.** Illness, exams, a bad week. Instead of a reset or a missed-day counter, we agreed on a pause: nothing counts, nothing is lost, and the return is "welcome back," not "what happened."
>
> I am a product manager by trade, and — full disclosure — I eventually built an app around those three ideas, with my son co-designing the teen interface, because nothing on the market would let a fifteen-year-old with ADHD keep his dignity. But the part I would defend on any stage has nothing to do with software. It is the stated goal we wrote at the top of the design document: *until they don't need us.* The measure of success is a kid who no longer needs the tool — or the parent standing in the doorway.
>
> Last spring, on a Tuesday, he was dressed before I came in. I stood in the hallway holding a sentence I didn't need to say. That is the whole story. Complicated, connected, and — on that one morning — quiet.

```
Hi Penny,

I'm Adi Elgarat German — senior PM, mentor, and mom of a 15-year-old with ADHD. Episode 302 crystallized something I'd struggled to articulate about why every ADHD app my son tried failed by month two: they're box-shaped. Deficit-correction tools. The kid sees through it in weeks.

Full disclosure — I'm the founder of BUFF, an ADHD coaching app for ages 6–18, and I'm pitching you a topic, not a sponsorship. Two design choices came straight from the strength-based frame: the reward is something real the child chose (a concert, an outing), never coins; and a good day is about three tasks done — never 100%, never a percentage. The unusual third thing, and why I think there's an episode in it: our written success metric is a kid who no longer needs the app. Most ADHD apps optimize against that. We optimize for it.

adi@buffadhd.com
```
